# Adversarial review — Rebechi, Van Kerm, Paradowski, Lepinteur & Rohde (2026), "What Can Money Buy? Inequality and Fiscal Policy Implications of Citizens United v. FEC"

Prepared for the IARIW 2026 discussion (Fri 28 Aug 2026, 4:00–5:30pm, Room A1, session "Recent Laws and their Inequality Implications"; discussant Max Ghenis presents, Philippe Van Kerm responds). Draft reviewed: 31 Jul 2026 conference version, 68 pp.

**Method.** Eight reviewer lenses (classification, replicability, net-partisan/mechanism, literature, econometrics, magnitudes, red-team, citations) read the full paper and fetched every external source they cite; a separate lane rebuilt the paper's pipeline from public data (`replication/`). Status legend: **CONFIRMED** = checked against the paper's own tables/text, our computations, or primary sources retrieved and quoted this session; **PLAUSIBLE** = supported but one link is interpretive or not independently re-checked; **REFUTED** = tried and did not survive. Long-form evidence per lens is in `review/lenses/`; every external paper cited below was retrieved (abstract or full text) on 22–23 Aug 2026 — none is from memory.

## Bottom line

The paper's numbers are honest and reproducible: from the public SCF 2010 extract and NBER TAXSIM-35 we re-derive Table 4 within sampling noise (top-1% ATR −0.67** vs their −0.53**; Reynolds–Smolensky −0.09** vs −0.11**; triple-difference −0.87** vs −0.83**), including the corporate-only arm's positive non-result and the Table B-1 amplification, and the stars survive randomization inference and a wild cluster bootstrap. What does not survive scrutiny is the interpretation. (1) The effect exists only where the **union** ban was also lifted, the corporate-only states move the other way, and the paper's own by-ban-type first stage (Table A-4) puts the political shift in the wrong group — while the one published paper that split this experiment by ban type (Gilens, Patterson & Haines, APSR 2021, uncited) found the heterogeneity in the **opposite** direction. (2) The treated–control gap was widening before 2010 at a comparable pace; the authors' own state-trend specification erases the effect, and both prior tax studies of this experiment abandoned level-DiD for exactly this reason. (3) The coefficient is a 13-state mean carried by five reform episodes (OH, ND, NC, RI, OK); three were legislated before any CU-affected legislature sat, and dropping NC+ND+OH leaves −0.29 (p = 0.22). The abstract describes a pooled effect the paper never estimates; the implied pooled estimate (≈ −0.2 pp, n.s.) is Slattery et al.'s published null.

## 1. Replication from public data (`replication/REPORT.md`)

Pipeline: SCF 2010 public summary extract (6,482 families, implicate 1, SCF weights; rescaled from the Fed's 2022-dollar convention) → NBER TAXSIM-35 run locally (R `usincometaxes` 0.7.1, WebAssembly) for 50 states + DC × 2004–2021 (≈ 5.9M simulated returns; CPI-U verified against BLS) → the paper's outcomes → eq. (7) with state and year FE, SEs clustered by state. Wall-clock ≈ 25 minutes of compute. All coefficients × 100.

| Table 4 row (corporate-&-union ban unless noted) | Paper | Replication |
|---|---|---|
| ATR top 1% | −0.53** (0.24) | −0.67** (0.29) |
| ATR top 5% | −0.33 (0.20) | −0.58** (0.25) |
| ATR top 1% by net worth | −0.36* (0.19) | −0.66** (0.28) |
| Reynolds–Smolensky | −0.11** (0.04) | −0.09** (0.03) |
| Difference (both − corp-only), top 1% | −0.83** (0.35) | −0.87** (0.38) |
| Corporate-only arm (all six outcomes) | positive, n.s. | positive, n.s. |
| Table B-1 (income-tax states only), top 1% | −0.77** (0.30) | −0.95** (0.36) |
| Overall ATR | −0.01 (0.13) | −0.35** (0.17) |

- N = 846 is reproduced **only with DC in the panel** (47 × 18); without DC, N = 828 and nothing material changes. Dating CU from 2011 changes nothing.
- Inference: cluster-robust p = 0.02 (top 1%) / 0.01 (RS); randomization inference over the 8/13 ban labels, 5,000 draws: p = 0.013 / 0.013; wild cluster bootstrap (Rademacher, 9,999): p = 0.030 / 0.016. **The stars are not a few-cluster artifact.**
- What does not reproduce: the clean zero on the overall ATR. The public extract cannot itemize deductions and taxes Social Security as pensions, which inflates middle-class liabilities so across-the-board cuts move our overall ATR; the top-1% and RS rows are insensitive to this. One implicate only; TAXSIM vintage differs from whatever the authors ran (the paper does not say).
- Added analyses: per-state contributions, leave-three-out, group-mean level series, event-study leads/lags, a household × state × year incidence regression, the paper's unreported β, and a union-density moderator (all in `replication/results/`).

## 3. Questions worth raising as discussant (ranked by how much they would change the conclusion)

1. **What is the treatment?** Your effect exists only where the union ban was also lifted (Table 4), your own by-ban-type first stage puts the political shift in the corporate-only group (Table A-4), and Gilens et al. (2021) — using the same experiment — found the opposite heterogeneity with the opposite union logic stated ex ante. Is "corporate-and-union ban" a treatment margin, or a label for the 2010 redistricting battlegrounds (MI OH PA TX NC WI) where Republican trifectas then cut taxes? A union-density dose-response test (we find none) and a named theory of change would settle it.
2. **Which −0.53 do you believe?** The treated–control gap widened as fast before 2010 as after; your trends specification takes the effect to ≈ 0; SDID group estimates are n.s.; Slattery et al. and Gilens et al. both abandoned level-DiD on this experiment for this reason. A Rambachan–Roth sensitivity bound, and reporting the trends spec as co-equal, would tell readers how much of the effect is trend.
3. **Five reforms or thirteen states?** OH, ND, NC, RI, OK carry the estimate; ND (May 2009, oil-financed), RI (June 2010) and OH (2005 law) were legislated before a CU-affected legislature sat; four treated states have no income tax. Report the per-state decomposition, leave-three-out (−0.29, p = 0.22 in our replication), and make the income-tax-states sample (Table B-1) the main one.
4. **Reconcile with the published record.** The pooled effect you never report is ≈ −0.2 pp (n.s.) — Slattery et al.'s null — and "most research finds no policy impact" is wrong (Gilens, Werner–Coleman, Akey). State the conditionality in the abstract; cite and confront Gilens et al.
5. **Model the tax function, not six summaries of it.** On the same 900 datasets: report β (your most precise outcome), the dollar incidence by filer type (bottom quintile ≈ 0, top 1% ≈ −$6–9k/yr), and state the SCF wave, income year, DC, implicates and TAXSIM build — then release the 47 × 18 panel and code. The result would be reproducible in an afternoon.

## 4. What held up

The headline replicates from public data with the same signs, magnitudes and stars; the corp-only null matches Slattery et al.'s corporate-only null; the stars survive randomization inference and a wild cluster bootstrap; the 13/8 classification follows Klumpp et al./NCSL (NH is the only contestable case and cannot move a tax outcome); the fixed-sample SCF → TAXSIM design is a clean de jure policy panel that the public SCF makes fully replicable; and the authors already disclose the two facts that matter most — that state trends attenuate the effect (Fig. 4) and that a few states drive it (p. 27). The discussion's job is to make the abstract say what the tables say.
