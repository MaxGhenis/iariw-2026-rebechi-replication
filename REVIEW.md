# Adversarial review — Rebechi, Van Kerm, Paradowski, Lepinteur & Rohde (2026), "What Can Money Buy? Inequality and Fiscal Policy Implications of Citizens United v. FEC"

Prepared for the IARIW 2026 discussion (Fri 28 Aug 2026, 4:00–5:30pm, Room A1, session "Recent Laws and their Inequality Implications"; discussant Max Ghenis presents, Philippe Van Kerm responds). Draft reviewed: 31 Jul 2026 conference version, 68 pp.

**Method.** Eight reviewer lenses (classification, replicability, net-partisan/mechanism, literature, econometrics, magnitudes, red-team, citations) read the full paper and fetched every external source they cite; a separate lane rebuilt the paper's pipeline from public data (`replication/`); and an independent GPT-5.6 audit (`review/VERIFY.md`) then tried to refute every finding and every slide number against the paper text, the downloaded sources and a re-estimation of the replication panel — 37 claims: 13 confirmed outright, 22 confirmed with a qualification (all applied below), 2 refuted (one was the memo's own refuted hypothesis; the other is re-stated as B4). Status legend: **CONFIRMED** = checked against the paper's own tables/text, our computations, or primary sources retrieved and quoted this session, and upheld by the audit; **PLAUSIBLE** = supported but one link is interpretive, figure-dependent, or not independently re-run; **REFUTED** = tried and did not survive. Long-form evidence per lens is in `review/lenses/`; every external paper cited below was retrieved (abstract or full text) on 22–23 Aug 2026 — none is from memory.

## Bottom line

The paper's numbers are honest and reproducible: from the public SCF 2010 extract and NBER TAXSIM-35 we re-derive Table 4 within sampling noise (top-1% ATR −0.67** vs their −0.53**; Reynolds–Smolensky −0.09** vs −0.11**; triple-difference −0.87** vs −0.83**), including the corporate-only arm's positive non-result and the Table B-1 amplification, and the stars survive randomization inference and a wild cluster bootstrap. What does not survive scrutiny is the interpretation. (1) The effect exists only where the **union** ban was also lifted, the corporate-only states move the other way, and the paper's own by-ban-type first stage (Table A-4) puts the political shift in the wrong group — while the one published paper that split this experiment by ban type (Gilens, Patterson & Haines, APSR 2021, uncited) found the heterogeneity in the **opposite** direction. (2) The treated–control gap was widening before 2010 at a comparable pace; the authors' own state-trend specification attenuates the effect toward zero (on the public panel: −0.27, p = 0.15), Slattery et al. explicitly report that parallel trends fail in levels of tax rates for this experiment, and Gilens et al. use an estimator that does not require them. (3) The coefficient is a 13-state mean carried by five reform episodes (OH, ND, NC, RI, OK); three were legislated before any CU-affected legislature sat, and dropping NC+ND+OH leaves −0.29 (p = 0.22). The abstract describes a pooled effect the paper never estimates; the implied pooled point (≈ −0.2 pp; public-panel pooled regression −0.34, SE 0.25, p = 0.19) is consistent with Slattery et al.'s published null.

(7) with state and year FE, SEs clustered by state. Wall-clock ≈ 25 minutes of compute. All coefficients × 100.

- N = 846 is reproduced **only with DC in the panel** (47 × 18); without DC, N = 828 and nothing material changes. Dating CU from 2011 changes nothing.
- Inference: cluster-robust p = 0.02 (top 1%) / 0.01 (RS); randomization inference over the 8/13 ban labels, 5,000 draws: p = 0.013 / 0.013; wild cluster bootstrap (Rademacher, 9,999): p = 0.030 / 0.016. **The stars are not a few-cluster artifact.**
- What does not reproduce: the clean zero on the overall ATR. The public extract cannot itemize deductions and taxes Social Security as pensions, which inflates middle-class liabilities so across-the-board cuts move our overall ATR; the top-1% and RS rows are insensitive to this. One implicate only; TAXSIM vintage differs from whatever the authors ran (the paper does not say).
- Added analyses: per-state contributions, leave-three-out, group-mean level series, event-study leads/lags, a household × state × year incidence regression, the paper's unreported β, and a union-density moderator (all in `replication/results/`).

## 3. Questions worth raising as discussant (ranked by how much they would change the conclusion)

1. **What is the treatment?** Your effect exists only where the union ban was also lifted (Table 4), your own by-ban-type first stage puts the political shift in the corporate-only group (Table A-4), and Gilens et al. (2021) — using the same experiment — found the opposite heterogeneity with the opposite union logic stated ex ante. Is "corporate-and-union ban" a treatment margin, or a label for the 2010 redistricting battlegrounds (MI OH PA TX NC WI) where Republican trifectas then cut taxes? A union-density dose-response test (we find none) and a named theory of change would settle it.
2. **Which −0.53 do you believe?** The treated–control gap widened at least as fast before 2010 as after; your trends specification — which you report — takes the effect close to zero (−0.27, n.s., on the public panel); SDID group estimates are n.s.; Slattery et al. explicitly reject parallel trends in levels for this experiment and use logs, and Gilens et al. use an estimator that does not need them. A Rambachan–Roth sensitivity bound, and reporting the trends spec as co-equal, would tell readers how much of the effect is trend.
3. **Five reforms or thirteen states?** You show the state heterogeneity (Fig. 5), LOO and B-1; what is missing is the joint picture: OH, ND, NC, RI, OK carry the estimate; ND (May 2009, during the oil boom — a plausible confounder), RI (June 2010) and OH (2005 law) were legislated before a CU-affected legislature sat; four treated states have no broad income tax. Report the per-state decomposition and leave-three-out (−0.29, p = 0.22 in our replication), and consider making the income-tax-states sample (Table B-1) the main one.
4. **Reconcile with the published record.** The pooled effect you never report is ≈ −0.2 pp (n.s.; −0.34, p = 0.19 on the public panel) — consistent with Slattery et al.'s null — and "most research finds no policy impact" overlooks Gilens, Werner–Coleman and Akey (the policy literature is mixed). State the conditionality in the abstract; cite and confront Gilens et al.
5. **Model the tax function, not six summaries of it.** On the same 900 datasets: report β (your most precise outcome), the dollar incidence by filer type (public-replication estimates: bottom quintile ≈ 0, top 1% ≈ −$6–9k/yr), say whether B is populated, and state the SCF wave, income year, DC, implicates and TAXSIM build — then release the 47 × 18 panel and code. The state-year regressions would be reproducible in an afternoon.

## 4. What held up

The headline replicates from public data with the same signs, magnitudes and stars (the overall-ATR zero and the Table 5 joint post-period tests do not); the corp-only null matches Slattery et al.'s corporate-only null; the stars survive randomization inference and a wild cluster bootstrap; the 13/8 classification follows the Klumpp et al. map with stated exclusions (NH is the only contestable case and cannot move a tax outcome); the fixed-sample SCF → TAXSIM design is a clean de jure policy panel that the public SCF makes fully replicable; and the authors already disclose the facts that matter most — that state trends attenuate the effect (Fig. 4), that SDID group effects are n.s., that the by-ban-type first stage is weak, and that a few states drive it (p. 27). The discussion's job is to make the abstract say what the tables say, and to ask what the treatment is.
