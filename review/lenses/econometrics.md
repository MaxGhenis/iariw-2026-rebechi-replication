# Design & econometrics assessment — Rebechi, Van Kerm, Paradowski, Lepinteur & Rohde (2026), "What Can Money Buy?"

Lens: identification and inference. All paper numbers cite table/figure + page (PDF page in parentheses where it differs). "Replication" = the from-scratch public-data replication in `replication/` of this worktree (SCF 2010 public extract + NBER TAXSIM-35 run locally, 47 units × 18 years, N=846), which reproduces the paper's Table 4 headline closely (top-1% β2: −0.67 (0.29) vs paper −0.53 (0.24); RS −0.089 (0.035) vs −0.11 (0.04); diff top-1% −0.87 vs −0.83). External claims verified this session via WebSearch/WebFetch are marked [VERIFIED, source]. Anything not verified is labeled.

## Verdict in one paragraph

The paper's numbers are real and reproducible, and the authors are unusually candid about heterogeneity ("Most of our results are driven by few outlying states", p.27). But the causal claim sits on three legs that each bend under weight: (1) the treated–control gap was already widening before 2010, at roughly *twice* the post-2010 rate, so a linear differential trend fitted to the pre-period alone reproduces (indeed overshoots) the headline coefficient — which is why the paper's own state-trend spec (Fig 4) and SDID (Table 6) attenuate it to insignificance; (2) the effect is a mean over 13 states carried by ~5 reform episodes, of which four (OH, ND, RI, OK) have major components legislated *before* Citizens United or before any post-CU election, and dropping the top three states kills significance (β2 = −0.29, p = 0.22); (3) the mechanism chain has no first stage: the paper's own Table A-4 shows the political shift does *not* differ by ban type, no state-level spending data appear anywhere in the paper, and union density neither differs much across groups pre-2010 nor moderates the effect within the treated group. What survives is a well-measured descriptive fact — simulated statutory progressivity fell after 2010 in a set of Republican-trending, corporate-and-union-ban states relative to controls — plus a genuinely interesting asymmetry (corp-only states moved the *other* way) that the abstract currently buries.

## Finding 1 (identification, HIGH). The pre-existing differential trend fully accounts for the headline estimate

**Claim.** The corp&union top-1% and RS effects are quantitatively consistent with the continuation — in fact the *deceleration* — of a differential trend that predates 2010. The design cannot distinguish "gradual treatment effect" from "pre-existing trajectory," and every specification that allows for a trend erases the result.

**Evidence.**
- *The paper's own event study* (Fig 3b, PDF 18): the corp&union pre-2010 coefficients are visibly positive early and decline monotonically into the omitted year 2009. The replication puts numbers on it: +0.465 (2004), +0.476 (2005), +0.453 (2006), +0.374 (2007), +0.223 (2008), 0 (2009) — a monotone slide of ≈ −0.09 pp/yr *into* treatment (`replication/results/event_study.csv`).
- *Raw levels* (Fig C-10b, PDF 62): the treated corp&union top-1% ATR falls near-linearly from ≈3.9 (2004) to ≈2.9 (2021) while the synthetic control is flat at ≈5.1–5.3. There is no visible break at the treatment line. Replication raw gap: −1.56 (2004) → −2.03 (2009) → −2.55 (2021): −0.078/yr pre vs −0.043/yr post. **The differential trend was ~2× steeper before CU than after.** A causal reading requires the "effect" of removing spending bans to have *decelerated* the pre-existing convergence — no theory in the paper predicts that.
- *Arithmetic*: with a single adoption date, TWFE β2 = (mean post event-coef) − (mean pre event-coef). A linear trend s fitted by OLS to the six pre-period event coefficients gives s = −0.090/yr (top-1%) and −0.011/yr (RS); pure continuation implies β2 = 9s = **−0.81 (top-1%) and −0.100 (RS)** — as large as or larger than the estimated −0.53/−0.67 and −0.11/−0.089. Net of the fitted pre-trend, the post-period residual is ≈ **+0.14**, i.e., wrong-signed for the paper's story. This is exactly what the paper's own Fig 4 (state-specific trends, PDF 19) shows: corp&union effects "substantially attenuated and move much closer to zero" (p.15) — and what SDID shows in milder form (−0.53 → −0.40, n.s.; Table 6, PDF 21), since SDID's λ weights load on late pre-years but no convex combination of *flat* controls can match a *declining* treated path.
- *The pre-test is uninformative, not reassuring.* Table 5 (PDF 18) pre-period joint p-values (corp&union: top-1% 0.166, RS 0.102) fail to reject — but with 13 treated clusters and individual-lead SEs of 0.14–0.26, a drift of 0.09/yr is essentially undetectable. This is precisely Roth's (2022) point that conventional pre-tests have low power and conditioning on passing them distorts inference [VERIFIED: Roth, "Pretest with Caution," AER: Insights 4(3), 2022, 305–22, aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aeri.20210236].

**The authors' defense and why it only half-works.** They argue (p.15) that state-specific trends "may also absorb genuine treatment-related variation" — true in general (gradual effects load on trends). But that argument cuts symmetrically: it means the design has no power to separate the two hypotheses, not that the no-trend estimate is preferred. And the deceleration fact (pre-slope > post-slope) is affirmative evidence *for* the trend story, which the "trends absorb effects" defense cannot explain.

**What would settle it.**
1. Rambachan–Roth honest-DiD sensitivity [VERIFIED: "A More Credible Approach to Parallel Trends," REStud 90(5), 2023, 2555–91]: report the breakdown parameter. Given pre-slope ≥ post-slope, the relative-magnitudes breakdown value is very likely below M̄ = 1 in the first post-years and near zero for the average effect (we computed the implied trend; running `HonestDiD` on their vcov is a one-afternoon exercise the authors should report).
2. Report the slope-change test directly: post-period slope minus pre-period slope of the treated–control gap (a "trend-break" parameter). It is positive (+0.045/yr) here — the wrong sign.
3. Freyaldenhoven–Hansen–Shapiro covariate/2SLS approach if a confound proxy (e.g., oil & gas severance revenue, lagged partisan control) can be excluded from the direct effect [VERIFIED: AER 109(9), 2019, 3307–38].
4. In-time placebo: assign CU to 2007; with a smooth trend the "placebo effect" will be significant.

## Finding 2 (identification, HIGH). The treatment group is the 2010 Republican wave + an oil boom; the effect is ~5 episodes, 4 of which were (partly) legislated pre-CU

**Claim.** "Lifting the union ban" is observationally a label for a bloc of Republican-trending states whose tax cuts have documented proximate causes — several predating CU — so β2 measures state-type divergence, not the incremental effect of union-ban removal.

**Evidence.**
- *Concentration* (replication `per_state_did.csv`, consistent with the paper's own Fig 5, PDF 22, and Figs B-1/B-2): per-state DiDs vs never-ban controls, top-1% ATR (pp): **OH −2.01, ND −1.98, NC −1.83, RI −1.73, OK −1.00**; then AZ −0.23; AK/TX/WY/NH/PA ≈ −0.07 (five of the 13 states have no or near-flat income tax and mechanically cannot respond); **MI +0.13 and WI +0.27 are opposite-signed** — notable because Michigan and Wisconsin are the poster children of the 2011 anti-union turn. The 13-state mean (= β2) is −0.67. Leave-one-out keeps β2 in [−0.75, −0.56] — the paper's LOO claim (App. B: results "do not depend on any single treated state") is technically true — but **dropping NC+ND+OH jointly: β2 = −0.29 (SE 0.24, p = 0.22); RS −0.039 (p = 0.12)** (`leave_out.csv`, `inference_summary.csv`).
- *Episode timing* (all verified this session):
  - **Ohio**: HB 66 (June 2005) legislated a 21% across-the-board income-tax cut phased 4.2%/yr over 2005–2009; the final tranche was delayed in 2009 to **2011** for recession reasons — so Ohio's early post-period decline executes a **2005 law** [VERIFIED: Tax Foundation "Ohio's Commercial Activity Tax: A Reappraisal" 2017; Ohio Dept. of Taxation individual income tax history]. Kasich's 2013+ cuts add a post-CU component.
  - **North Dakota**: rate cuts began **2009** — pre-CU — "during the first bloom of the oil boom," repeated 2011/2013, roughly halving rates (top 2.9%) [VERIFIED: Minot Daily News 2017; tax.nd.gov individual income tax history]. The driver on the record is oil revenue.
  - **Rhode Island**: H 8196, cutting the top rate 9.9% → 5.99% effective 2011, passed the (Democratic-supermajority) General Assembly **unanimously on June 4, 2010** — before RI's own CU compliance date (July 13, 2010; paper Table 2, p.8) and five months before the first post-CU election [VERIFIED: Lexology summary of the Act; Tax Foundation]. RI is the fourth-largest contributor (−1.73). No electoral CU channel can reach it.
  - **Oklahoma**: bipartisan 2004–2007 legislation cut the top rate 6.65% → 5.5% by 2009 and included a **pre-legislated trigger that automatically cut the rate to 5.25% in 2012**; a 2014 law took it to 5.0% [VERIFIED: Oklahoma Policy Institute, okpolicy.org]. So OK's pre-period decline and part of its post-period decline are mid-2000s law.
  - **North Carolina**: the 2013 flat-tax reform (7.75 → 5.8 in 2014; simulated top-1% ATR 7.35 → 5.69 in the replication) is the one clean post-CU-election episode — GOP won both chambers in 2010 "for the first time since Reconstruction," trifecta 2013 [VERIFIED: Ballotpedia, 2010 state legislative elections].
- *Wave concentration*: the 2010 election created GOP trifectas in AL, FL, IN, KS, ME, MI, OH, OK, PA, TN, WI, WY [VERIFIED: Ballotpedia "State legislative elections, 2010" / "A look back at the 2010 state legislative elections"]. That is **6 of 13 corp&union states (MI OH OK PA WI WY) vs 5 of 26 never-ban controls vs 1 of 8 corp-only (TN)**. The paper's own Fig C-1 (PDF 54) and App. A.1 text concede the corp&union group is "disproportionately Republican-controlled." Year FE absorb a *national* wave, not a wave concentrated in the treated group.
- The authors can reply that the wave concentration *is* the treatment effect — Klumpp, Mialon & Williams find CU raised Republican win probability in ban states by ≈4pp in state house races [VERIFIED: JLE 59(1), 2016]. But their own Table A-4 (PDF 41) undercuts that reply for the *contrast that identifies the paper*: the political effect does not differ by ban type (Finding 4), while the tax effect allegedly does.

**Fixes.** (i) Placebo among never-ban states: interact post-2010 with "gained GOP trifecta in 2010–2013" (Kansas — Brownback 2012–13 cuts, visible in the replication's simulated series — Maine, Florida, Indiana, Alabama); if the placebo replicates the pattern, the exclusion restriction fails. (ii) Re-date each driver: code reform streams by *enactment* date and legislative session, and drop components legislated pre-2010 (OH 2011 tranche, OK 2012 trigger, RI June-2010 act); report how much of β2 survives. (iii) Control for oil & gas severance revenue × year (ND, AK, TX, WY, OK are all extraction states). (iv) Region × year FE as a coarse wave absorber.

## Finding 3 (overclaiming, MEDIUM-HIGH). The abstract buries the lede, and the pooled claim collides with Slattery et al.'s published null on the same experiment

**Claim.** The abstract ("lifting restrictions on independent expenditures led to larger reductions in top income tax rates and a decline in overall tax progressivity than in control states") states a pooled treatment effect the paper does not find; the significant effect exists only in the corp&union arm, the corp-only arm is *positive* (n.s.), and the identifying contrast is a between-treated-groups difference — precisely the contrast that the closest published paper, using the same natural experiment with statutory outcomes, does not support as a pooled effect.

**Evidence.**
- Table 4 (PDF 17): corp-only top-1% **+0.30** (0.30), RS +0.07; corp&union −0.53** (0.24), RS −0.11**; the "difference in CU effects" −0.83** is what carries the stars, and about a third of it is the positive corp-only point estimate. A back-of-envelope pooled effect is (8×0.30 + 13×(−0.53))/21 ≈ −0.22 — plainly indistinguishable from zero. The paper's own Discussion says the corp-only pattern is "counterintuitive" and driven by CT and MN (p.19).
- Slattery, Tazhitdinova & Robinson (JPubE 221, 2023) studied the identical 23-ban-state experiment with statutory outcomes **including the top personal income tax rate**, pooling both ban types: "Across all outcomes, we find no statistically or economically significant effects of independent political contributions on tax outcomes," and they "reject effects on tax changes that are larger in magnitude than the average tax change implemented by states during business as usual" [VERIFIED: NBER w30352 abstract; Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance summary, Aug 31, 2022]. The present paper's fn 5 (p.9) characterizes this as "finding only a moderate effect of CU" — a **mischaracterization of a stated null** — and the Conclusion's "Most research to date has found no impact on actual policy. In contrast, we find evidence of a persistent impact" (p.28) elides that the *pooled* result here is also ≈ 0 and the novelty is entirely the split.
- Triple-diff logic: interpreting −0.83 as "the effect of *also* lifting the union ban" requires the corporate-lift effect to be homogeneous across the two groups of states — i.e., that CT/MA/MN-type states are the right counterfactual for TX/ND/OK-type states. The paper itself argues the groups differ systematically (union strength, partisanship, Fig C-1) — which is an argument *against* the homogeneity needed for that reading. As stated, β2 − β1 is a descriptive contrast between two non-randomly different blocs of states.
- Also within the union-counterweight story the corp-only arm is anomalous in sign: in corp-only states unions were already free pre-2010 and *gained nothing* from CU while corporations gained everything — the raw prediction is cuts there too, not the observed (CT/MN-driven) increases.

**Fix.** Rewrite the abstract around the asymmetry: "we find no effect where only corporate spending had been banned (point estimates positive); simulated top-1% tax rates and progressivity fell only in states that had banned both corporate and union spending; the difference is significant." Correct fn 5. Explicitly reconcile with STR 2023: statutory-rate outcomes vs simulated-ATR outcomes on the same variation, pooled vs split — a table would do it.

## Finding 4 (mechanism, MEDIUM-HIGH). The mechanism chain has no first stage: politics doesn't differ by ban type, no state-level spending is measured, and union density doesn't moderate

**Claim.** The paper's causal chain (CU → outside money → rightward political shift → tax cuts, strongest where unions were also unbanned) fails at each measured link for the contrast that identifies the paper.

**Evidence.**
- *Political first stage by ban type* (Table A-4, PDF 41): Republican vote share — corp-only +0.05 (0.03), corp&union **+0.01 (0.02)**, difference −0.04 (0.03) n.s.; median ideology H — corp-only +0.27**, corp&union +0.18 n.s.; S — +0.25* vs +0.23 n.s.; polarization n.s.; **all differences n.s., and where anything is significant it is in the *corp-only* group** — the group with the *smaller* (wrong-signed) tax effect. The paper concedes this ("a positive and statistically significant shift in median legislative ideology only in states that... banned corporate... but not union" — p.24) but does not confront the implication: the tax-effect asymmetry cannot be transmitted by the measured political shift.
- *Residualization* (Table 8, PDF 26): effects persist essentially unchanged after partialling Republican vote share/ideology/polarization (top-1% −0.47 to −0.56). The authors read this as "additional mechanisms — lobbying, donor pressure, agenda-setting" (p.24). The sceptic's equal-fit reading: the political variables are not what drives the treated–control divergence, because the divergence is the pre-existing trajectory of Finding 1. Residualizing on a mediator that never differed by ban type (A-4) mechanically leaves the effect intact.
- *No money is measured.* Tables 1 and 3 (pp.7, 9) are *national federal* OpenSecrets totals. No state-level independent-expenditure series appears anywhere, so the "dose" of the treatment is never observed — for the treated states, the control states, or the driver episodes (who spent what in NC/OH/ND legislative races?). The cited Spencer & Wood (2014) built exactly such state-level spending data.
- *Union counterweight is thin.* Fig 6 (PDF 28): pre-2010 union density ≈ 12.2–12.7% (corp-only) vs ≈ 11.5% (corp&union) vs ≈ 10.8–11% (no ban) — a ~1pp gap, with the corp&union group *at or above the controls* (the replication's Hirsch–Macpherson panel: 12.4 / 11.5 / 11.5). Replication moderator analysis (`union_density_moderator.csv`): CU × pre-2010 density enters *positive* (+0.065 per density point, p ≈ 0.003 — high-union states *raised* top taxes post-2010, consistent with CT/MN); adding it leaves the ban coefficients unchanged (−0.674 → −0.677); and within the corp&union group the effect does **not** scale with density (interaction −0.023, p = 0.57). No dose-response for a "silenced/outgunned unions" story.

**Fixes.** Bring actual expenditure data (FollowTheMoney/OpenSecrets state-level independent spending), even descriptively for the five driver states; test the union story as effect modification by *density* rather than by legal ban type (the two make different predictions — density says CT/MN, ban type says TX/WY); and connect specific reforms to specific spenders (the NC 2013 episode has a rich documentary record).

## Finding 5 (overclaiming/inference, MEDIUM). "Robust to SDID" and "robust to Wooldridge" overstate; the two named robustness checks are one insignificant estimator and one algebraic identity

**Claim.** Of the paper's two headline robustness exercises, SDID does not confirm the group-level result (it attenuates it to insignificance), and the "alternative TWFE estimator... proposed by Wooldridge (2025)" is numerically the same estimator, so Table C-1 contains no independent information.

**Evidence.**
- Table 6 (PDF 21): SDID corp&union top-1% **−0.40 (0.25) n.s.**, RS **−0.07 (0.05) n.s.**, top5 −0.29 (0.19) n.s.; only the cross-group differences reach 10%: top-1% −0.72* (0.38), RS −0.13* (0.08), with SEs "computed under the assumption of independence across estimates" (their note) from 1,000 placebo reassignments. The two estimates share the same 26 control states, so their errors are positively correlated (common control shocks enter both with the same sign); independence overstates Var(diff) — conservative in that one respect — but the placebo-variance method itself is fragile with 8 treated units, and none of this rescues the group-level insignificance. Text: "The results broadly confirm our main findings" (p.16) — the sign survives; the significance does not. B-2 (income-tax states only, PDF 51) recovers −0.57* at 10% for top-1%, RS −0.08 n.s.
- The SDID attenuation is itself diagnostic: reweighting toward the late pre-period (λ weights concentrated 2007–2010 in Figs C-9/C-10) mechanically strips part of a linear differential trend — and the estimate drops 25%. An estimator that better respects pre-trends finds less effect (Finding 1 again).
- Table C-1 (PDF 53): the "Wooldridge (2025)" estimates are **identical to Table 4 to the last digit** (0.01/0.14/0.30/0.07/0.10/0.07; −0.01/−0.33/−0.53/−0.25/−0.36/−0.11) — necessarily, since Wooldridge's central result is that the two-way Mundlak regression is *numerically equivalent* to TWFE [VERIFIED: Wooldridge, Empirical Economics 69(5), 2025, 2545–87; SSRN 3906345 abstract: equivalence of TWFE and TWM]. With a single adoption date and no added covariates or heterogeneity, the "alternative estimation" is the same estimation. (Same for Table D-2 vs D-1.) The parenthetical on p.15 — "results are also confirmed using an alternative TWFE estimator" — is circular.
- App. B's leave-one-out framing ("do not depend on any single treated state") is accurate but answers the wrong question when the top three states jointly account for the significance (Finding 2).

**Fixes.** Estimate the two SDID effects jointly (or bootstrap the pair over control-state resamples) to get a real covariance for the difference; add SDID in-time placebos; either exploit Wooldridge's estimator for what it adds (heterogeneous effects by cohort/time, covariates) or drop C-1/D-2.

## Finding 6 (inference, MEDIUM — partly favorable to the paper). Few-treated-cluster corrections actually support Table 4's stars; the real inference issues are concentration, exchangeability, multiplicity, and a non-reproducible joint test

**Claim.** The generic "8 and 13 treated clusters among 47 — cluster-robust SEs over-reject" objection (MacKinnon–Webb, Conley–Taber) turns out *not* to overturn Table 4: on the replication panel, wild cluster bootstrap and randomization inference give p-values close to the clustered-t ones. The paper should report these (it currently reports only CRVE with 47 clusters), and the sharper problems are elsewhere.

**Evidence.**
- Replication (`inference_summary.csv`, `randomization_inference.csv`), corp&union β2 on the main sample: clustered t(46) p = 0.023 (top-1%) / 0.014 (RS); **wild cluster bootstrap (Rademacher, B = 9,999): p = 0.030 / 0.016**; **randomization inference permuting the 8/13 ban labels (5,000 draws): p = 0.013 / 0.013**; difference row RI p = 0.012 / 0.018. So the significance is not a few-cluster asymptotics artifact. Literature the paper should engage: MacKinnon & Webb (Econometrics J. 2018, "The wild bootstrap for few (treated) clusters"; J. Econometrics 218(2), 2020, randomization inference for DiD with few treated clusters) and Conley & Taber (REStat 93(1), 2011) [all VERIFIED via publisher/RePEc pages].
- *But*: (i) RI's validity requires exchangeability of ban labels across states, which Fig C-1 (partisan imbalance by ban type) itself contradicts — permuting labels onto, say, CA/NY produces placebo draws unlike the treated bloc, and RI cannot fix confounding, only sampling noise; (ii) the estimand is a group mean carried by 3–5 states — dropping NC+ND+OH: p = 0.22 (Finding 2) — so "significant" describes the bloc contrast, not a broad-based response ("a valid group-mean contrast, not a broad-based treatment response"); (iii) Table 4 reports 18 coefficient cells across 6 correlated outcomes with no multiplicity adjustment; a Romano–Wolf step-down within the outcome family would be cheap (the top-1%/RS results would likely survive; the single-starred nw rows may not); (iv) **the paper's Table 5 joint post-treatment tests could not be reproduced**: paper p = 0.025 (top-1%) and 0.049 (RS) vs replication p = 0.52 / 0.23 for the same joint test on a panel whose pooled coefficients match — the pooled TWFE is significant, the 12 post coefficients jointly are not, in the replication. Also Table 5's "Post diff." p = 0.000 for top5/top1 coexists with a pooled-difference p ≈ 0.02 — surprising; the test details (which coefficients, which vcov) should be shown.
- Spec hygiene (small, listable): "46 states" (p.12) vs N = 846 = 47 units × 18 years (DC is in the panel; replication: dropping DC changes nothing, −0.68); eq. (8) runs j = −6..+10 with 2009 omitted, but 2004–2021 data makes 2021 = +11 — as written the spec would pool 2021 with the baseline (Fig 3's series visibly extends to 2021, so presumably +11 exists and eq. 8 has a typo); "CU_t = 1 after 2010" (p.12) is ambiguous about 2010 itself while the event study treats 2010 as j = 0 (all compliance dates are Jan–Oct 2010; FY2011 budgets passed mid-2010; replication: dating from 2011 gives −0.65, immaterial); NH treated with no compliance date (paper flags this, Table 2 note).

**Fixes.** Report WCB and RI p-values in Table 4's notes; present per-state contributions in the main text (Fig 5 nearly does this) and the NC+ND+OH-drop estimate; add Romano–Wolf; publish the joint-test code; fix the eq. (8)/sample-count typos.

## Finding 7 (measurement/presentation, MEDIUM). What the outcome measures — and the free upgrade the fixed-sample design makes possible

**Claim.** The outcome is the *de jure* state income-tax schedule evaluated on one fixed national 2010 SCF sample — a clean object (no behavioral response, no composition drift, forced anyway by the public SCF's lack of state identifiers), but narrower than the paper's framing ("post-government inequality," eq. 1's benefits B — TAXSIM has no benefits; "top income tax rates" — these are ATRs). Two defined quantities are never reported (the β progressivity measure of eq. 5; any bottom-quintile burden despite the hypothesis "maintaining or increasing it for those at the bottom," p.9). Meanwhile the fixed-sample design supports a household-level incidence analysis the paper leaves on the table.

**Evidence & specifics.**
- *Magnitudes need two-sided framing*: −0.53pp on a treated top-1% ATR level of ≈3.4–3.9 (Fig C-10b) is a ~14–16% cut in the top-1% state income-tax burden — economically real. But RS(×100) −0.11 on a treated level of ≈0.42 is a change in the Gini reduction of **0.0011 Gini points** — state income taxes reduce the Gini by well under 0.01 to begin with. Both the "26% of state-tax redistribution" and the "0.001 Gini points" readings should appear.
- *Missing β and bottom quintile*: eq. (5) defines β = ATR(top20) − ATR(bottom20); it appears in no table. The replication computes the bottom-quintile effect: **+0.21pp (p = 0.13)** — point estimate consistent with the paper's own hypothesis, worth reporting either way.
- *Mechanical drift in the outcome*: incomes are CPI-scaled to year t (fn 8) while many states' brackets/exemptions are fixed in nominal law, so non-indexing states accrue simulated bracket creep as a smooth pseudo-trend; the control group's RS rises steadily 0.46 → 0.63 over 2004–2021 (Fig C-10e) even though few control states legislated progressivity increases — the corp&union "RS effect" is substantially "treated states failed to match the controls' *rising* simulated progressivity." How much of that control-side rise is passive non-indexation vs legislation is checkable from the TAXSIM parameter files and should be reported (question, not established fact).
- *Fixed-sample micro upgrade* (Max's suggestion, demonstrated in `replication/scripts/09_micro_heterogeneity.py`): because the identical households sit in every state-year cell, a household×state×year stacked regression with household-group × CU × ban-type interactions (state and year FE, state-clustered SEs) is *algebraically equivalent* to running the paper's own Φ on subgroups — no new data needed, and it converts index movements into incidence people understand. Replication estimates (corp&union β2): bottom 20% **+0.21** (n.s.), 20–80 −0.19, 80–95 −0.33*, 95–99 −0.48**, **top 1% −0.67** ≈ **−$9,014/yr per top-1% household** (mean AGI $1.34M, 2010$); net-worth top 1% −0.66 ≈ −$6,336; capital-share>50% households −0.53 vs wage-share>90% top-5% households −0.54 — *capital-heavy and wage-heavy top households are hit alike*, consistent with across-the-board top-rate cuts (OH/ND/NC/RI/OK) rather than capital carve-outs; age 65+ −0.31; married-with-kids −0.36. Caveats the authors should state: the equivalence means no new statistical power (inference stays at the state level; the micro sample is not independent across states); SCF weights and multiple implicates should be carried through (neither the paper nor the replication propagates implicate variance).
- *Framing*: retitle claims to "simulated statutory progressivity of state income taxes"; drop or implement B; reconcile "top income tax rates" (abstract) with ATRs (tables).

## Fairness ledger — what the authors already do right

- They disclose the concentration themselves ("Most of our results are driven by few outlying states," p.27) and show per-state SDID (Fig 5) and LOO (B-1/B-2).
- They present the state-trend attenuation rather than hiding it (Fig 4) and explicitly caution about the SDID difference SEs ("should be interpreted as approximate," Table 6 note).
- Event-study, SDID, joint tests, B-1 sample restriction, and an after-federal-tax variant (App. D) is a serious robustness battery by current applied standards; the B-1 amplification (−0.77**) replicates (−0.95**), and the no-income-tax-state attenuation logic is sound.
- The fixed-sample simulation design is a *strength* for measuring policy (it removes behavioral/composition confounds and is forced by SCF's lack of state IDs); the criticism is the framing, not the construction.
- Clustered SEs at the state level with uniform timing avoid the staggered-TWFE pathologies (they cite Baker et al. 2026); and the replication confirms their headline numbers are honestly computable from public data.

## Ranked bottom line for the slides

1. Pre-trend arithmetic (Finding 1) — the single most conclusion-threatening fact; pairs with the authors' own Fig 4.
2. Episode anatomy + wave confound (Finding 2) — RI (June 2010, unanimous), OH (2005 law), OK (mid-2000s trigger), ND (2009 oil) vs NC (the one clean case).
3. Abstract/lede + STR reconciliation (Finding 3) — Max's point (a).
4. No-first-stage mechanism (Finding 4) — Max's point (b): no theory of change is evidenced.
5. SDID/Wooldridge overclaim (Finding 5).
6. Inference: give them WCB/RI (it survives), take the joint-test and multiplicity issues (Finding 6).
7. Micro incidence offer (Finding 7) — Max's point (c): constructive close, "here's the paper you can write with the data you already have."
