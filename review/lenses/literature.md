# The empirical Citizens United literature — verified survey

Prepared for Max Ghenis's IARIW 2026 discussion of Rebechi, Van Kerm, Paradowski, Lepinteur & Rohde (2026), "What Can Money Buy?" All entries verified this session via WebSearch/WebFetch or full-text PDF download unless marked UNVERIFIED. Rebechi et al. page/table references are to the 31 Jul 2026 draft.

## Bottom lines

1. **The electoral first stage is well-established; the tax-policy step is where Rebechi et al. depart from the literature.** Klumpp et al. (2016), Abdul-Razzak et al. (2020), Harvey & Mattia (2022), Balles (2022/2024) and (federally) Cox (2024) all find pro-Republican/conservative electoral effects. But on taxes: Slattery et al. (2023) is a precise null (including the **top personal income tax rate**), Akey et al. (2023) find negative but insignificant tax-rate estimates, and Gilens et al. (2021) find only a small corporate-tax effect (contested by Slattery et al. on methodology).
2. **Every existing test of ban-type/union heterogeneity in policy runs opposite to, or fails to find, Rebechi et al.'s pattern.** Gilens et al. (2021): corporate-tax effect about TWICE as large in the 8 corporate-only-ban states (−5.55, p=0.001 vs −2.83, p=0.01) — "consistent with H3" (their prior that the corp-only shock is cleanest because unions there were never restricted). Slattery et al. (2023, App. D.1): restricting treatment to corporate-only states gives "a more precisely estimated null." Akey et al. (2023): treating corp&union states as treated and corp-only states as controls gives "no statistically significant impact ... across all economic outcomes." Rebechi et al. find the effect ONLY in corp&union states, with corp-only point estimates positive.
3. **Pooled, Rebechi et al.'s estimates are consistent with the published nulls; the headline rests entirely on the split.** State-count-weighted pooling of Table 4 (8 corp-only, 13 corp&union): top-1% ATR (8×0.30 + 13×(−0.53))/21 ≈ **−0.21pp**; RS ≈ −0.04; overall ATR ≈ 0.00. Against Slattery's treated-state mean top PIT rate of 6.51%, −0.21pp ≈ −3% — comfortably inside the >10–20% changes Slattery et al. can rule out (states' business-as-usual top-PIT changes averaged 12%, 18% over 5 years). The papers do not contradict each other in aggregate; the disagreement is confined to the ban-type disaggregation and its interpretation.
4. **"Most research to date has found no impact on actual policy" (Concl., p.27–28) is inaccurate as stated, and footnote 5's "Slattery et al. (2023) examines state-level corporate taxation, finding only a moderate effect" misstates a null and understates its scope.** Slattery's abstract: "we are not able to identify economically or statistically significant effects ... including tax rates, discretionary tax breaks, and tax revenues," and their tax rates include the top personal income tax rate. Meanwhile Gilens et al. (2021, APSR) DO find policy effects (corporate tax, tort), Werner & Coleman (2015, JLEO) find antitakeover effects, Akey et al. (2023) find reduced regulatory burden/enforcement, and Fordham (2024, LSQ) finds democratic-performance effects. The accurate statement: evidence on statutory tax parameters is null-to-small; evidence on lower-salience pro-business policy margins is mixed-positive.
5. **Mechanism evidence favors dark money and wealthy individuals over corporate treasuries.** Spencer & Wood (2014): the treated-state spending surge ran "nearly all ... through nonprofit organizations and political committees" with weak disclosure. Hansen, Rocca & Ortiz (2015): Fortune 500 firms were NOT the source of the 2012 surge. Petrova et al. (2019, unpublished; per APW fn.8): "no differential effect between Republicans and Democrats" in ads. Babenko et al. (2025): the top-1% DONOR share of contributions tripled post-CU and legislators' fiscal roll-call votes became more responsive to the wealthy — the strongest verified support for Rebechi's premise, but through mega-donors, not corporations.

## Answers to the assigned questions

**Where does Rebechi et al.'s identifying assumption sit relative to this evidence?** The assumption — that post-2010 divergence between ban and never-ban states is caused by the ruling — is the standard one (Klumpp; APW; Slattery; Gilens; Akey all use it). The literature supports it for ELECTORAL outcomes (parallel pre-trends, compliance-date timing). For TAX outcomes it is strained by three verified facts: (i) La Raja & Schaffner and Slattery's ban-introduction studies show ban ADOPTION had no detectable effects, implying ban states are politically distinctive for reasons other than the bans; (ii) the treated corp&union group is disproportionately Republican-leaning, low-union, and (per Rebechi's own Figs 6–7) on a differential post-2010 business-friendliness trajectory — i.e., the group's post-2010 tax cuts coincide with the post-2010 red-state tax-cut wave, and Rebechi's Fig 4 (state trends) attenuates the effects toward zero; (iii) every published attempt to isolate the union-ban margin (Gilens's split, Slattery's D.1, Akey's corp-only-as-controls design) finds either the OPPOSITE ordering or nothing.

**Which papers support / contradict the pro-Republican, pro-business net-effect premise?** Support: Klumpp et al. 2016; Abdul-Razzak et al. 2020; Harvey & Mattia 2022; Balles 2022/2024; Cox 2024 (on average, muted); Gilens et al. 2021 (policy); Babenko et al. 2025 (fiscal representation of the wealthy); Fordham 2024. Complicate or contradict: La Raja & Schaffner 2014 (bans never mattered); Hansen, Rocca & Ortiz 2015 (big corporations didn't spend); Petrova et al. 2019 per APW fn.8 (ads partisan-neutral); Akey et al. 2023 (turnover in both parties, polarization down, broad labor-income gains); Slattery et al. 2023 (no tax-policy effect); APW's own polarization null and preliminary public-goods null. No verified paper finds CU helped Democrats on net; the closest are partisan-neutral findings — though Rebechi's own Table 1 shows federal outside spending FOR Democrats exceeding spending FOR Republicans by 2020 and 2024.

**Is "Most research to date has found no impact on actual policy" accurate?** Only for statutory tax parameters (Slattery null; Akey n.s.; APW preliminary null on public goods; Werner & Coleman null on minimum wage). It is wrong about the broader policy literature: Gilens et al. (2021) find significant corporate-tax and tort effects in APSR; Werner & Coleman (2015) find antitakeover effects; Akey et al. (2023) find regulatory-burden reductions; Fordham (2024) finds institutional effects. And the sentence sits awkwardly next to fn.5, which converts Slattery's null into "a moderate effect." The defensible framing: *salient statutory tax parameters show precise nulls; low-salience pro-business margins show effects; Rebechi et al. claim the nulls hide offsetting ban-type heterogeneity — a claim whose sign contradicts the one published ban-type policy split (Gilens et al.).*
