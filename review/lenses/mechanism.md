# Mechanism & theory of change — Rebechi et al. (2026) Citizens United paper

Lens: net partisan effect of outside spending + does the paper establish its mechanism + theory of change in the driver states.
All external claims below verified this session (WebSearch/WebFetch/downloaded data) unless marked UNVERIFIED. Paper references cite table/figure/page of the 31 Jul 2026 draft.

## 1. Net partisan direction of post-CU outside spending (paper's own Table 1, p.7)

Party-aligned outside spending = "for own party" + "against other party" ($M, federal, OpenSecrets per the paper):

| Cycle | For D | For R | Against D | Against R | Pro-Dem (ForD+AntiR) | Pro-Rep (ForR+AntiD) | Pro-Rep share | Pro-Rep − Pro-Dem |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2004 | 60 | 23 | 4 | 7 | **67** | **27** | 28.7% | −40 |
| 2008 | 121 | 20 | 34 | 32 | **153** | **54** | 26.1% | −99 |
| 2012 | 73 | 179 | 472 | 295 | **368** | **651** | **63.9%** | +283 |
| 2016 | 150 | 343 | 392 | 504 | **654** | **735** | 52.9% | +81 |
| 2020 | 644 | 961 | 1,003 | 961 | **1,605** | **1,964** | 55.0% | +359 |
| 2024 | 1,062 | 763 | 1,411 | 923 | **1,985** | **2,174** | 52.3% | +189 |

- Pre-CU, outside money was **pro-Democratic** (71–74% of party-aligned spending, i.e., union/527 money: 2004 ACT/MoveOn era).
- Post-CU it flipped to a **modest Republican tilt**: 64% in 2012, then 53–55% — an arms race near parity, not one-way corporate capture. Within the paper's estimation window (outcomes through 2021 → cycles 2012–2020), the pro-R share is 64/53/55%.
- Growth 2004→2024: pro-Dem ×29.6 (67→1,985), pro-Rep ×80.5 (27→2,174). The paper headlines only "against Democrats ×353," which is a $4M-denominator artifact (2004 base).
- **These are FEDERAL totals** (presidential/congressional). The outcomes are STATE legislative tax policy. The paper presents no state-level outside-spending evidence at all — by party, by spender, or by treated/control status.
- Oddity worth double-checking with authors: 2020 "For Republicans" and "Against Republicans" are both exactly 961.

## 2. Who actually spent, at the state level (verified literature)

- **Spencer & Wood (2014, Indiana L.J. 89:315)** — the only direct state-level IE data (NIMSP/FollowTheMoney, 16 states, 2006–2010): IEs roughly **doubled (+100%)** in treated states relative to controls ($19M of the $32M treated-state increase attributable). Composition: **almost the entire increase went through 501(c) nonprofits and 527 committees** (+$25M, share +77%), with donors largely undisclosed. Direct **corporate treasury IEs were essentially zero**: across all their states, corporate spenders totaled ~$1,400 (control states) and ~$3,400 (treated states — one catered meal in Texas plus $100 in Alaska). **Union direct spending changed little (+4% in treated states; +5% ≈ $300k in the four corp-only states where it was always legal, on a $6.5M 2006 base)** (their fn.143). They also code **Washington as a corporate-ban treated state** (the paper's Klumpp-based map has WA as a never-ban control) and **drop North Carolina because its repeal took effect in 2011** (the paper codes NC treated July 10, 2010) — treatment-classification disagreement in the literature.
- **Hansen, Rocca & Ortiz (2015, J. Politics 77(2), doi:10.1086/680077)**: IEs rose 594% from 2008 to 2012 ($144M→$1B), but **Fortune 500 corporate spending did not change**; big public corporations "were not the source" of the increase (reputational/disclosure risk unchanged by CU).
- **Klumpp, Mialon & Williams (2016, J. Law & Econ 59(1))** — the paper's classification source: CU associated with **+4pp Republican win probability in state house races** (10+pp in several states). So a state-level partisan first stage exists in the literature — but see §3: the paper's own by-ban-type first stage doesn't line up.
- **Abdul-Razzak, Prato & Wolton (2020, Electoral Studies 67)**: outside spending increased GOP success/conservatism, with effects **conditional on union power and business-GOP alignment** — a *continuous* union-density interaction, which the ban-type split proxies badly (§4).
- **REDMAP (documented reporting: PBS, BillMoyers, Documented.net)**: the Republican State Leadership Committee spent **$30M** on 2010 state legislative races (107 races, 16 states, incl. PA, OH, MI, NC, WI — all corp&union-ban states), funded by corporate donors (Chevron $200k, Marathon $200k, Koch $101k, Wal-Mart $100k); GOP gained ~680–720 seats and ~20 chambers.
- **Union money post-CU in the high-union corp&union states** (all verified):
  - **Wisconsin recalls 2011–12** (candidate elections → post-CU IE rules apply; WI compliance May 10, 2010): total spending **$137.5M** (Wisconsin Democracy Campaign); the union coalition PAC **We Are Wisconsin led ALL outside groups with ~$10.8M of IEs** in the 2011 Senate recalls; Club for Growth WI ≥$1.5M TV.
  - **Ohio Issue 2 (Nov 2011, SB5 repeal)**: union side **We Are Ohio raised ~$30M — four times** Building a Better Ohio's corporate-side total; repeal won. (Ballot measure, so not a CU test per se — corporate/union treasury spending on ballot questions was already legal — but direct evidence on relative union vs corporate financial capacity in Ohio.)
  - **Michigan Prop 2 (2012)**: Protect Working Families **$23.66M** (UAW $3.56M, MEA $2.69M, AFL-CIO $1.84M, NEA $1.5M, AFT $1.0M) vs Protecting Michigan Taxpayers **$23.18M** (MI Chamber $9.2M, MI Alliance for Business Growth $5.5M, Adelson $2M, DeVos family $2M). Failed 57–43.

Bottom line: the documented post-CU state money is (a) opaque-intermediary money, not corporate treasuries; (b) two-sided, with unions among the largest outside spenders in exactly the corp&union states the paper describes as places where "corporations … were better positioned to expand political spending" (p.27).

## 3. Does the paper establish its mechanism? (its own tables say ~90% is unexplained)

Claimed chain: CU → more conservative/Republican legislatures → less progressive taxes.

- **First stage by ban type (Table A-4, p.A-8)**: Republican vote share: corp-only +0.05 (0.03) n.s., corp&union +0.01 (0.02) n.s. Median ideology: significant ONLY in corp-only states (H 0.27**, S 0.25*), corp&union n.s. (0.18, 0.23). Differences across ban types all n.s. **The politically-shifted group (corp-only) is the group with NO tax effect; the group with the tax effect (corp&union) has no significant political shift.**
- **Mediation (Table 8, p.25)**: residualizing the outcome on the political variables barely moves the corp&union top-1% coefficient: baseline −0.53** → **−0.56** (Rep vote share — LARGER), −0.47/−0.48 (ideology), −0.49/−0.51 (polarization). RS: −0.11 → −0.08/−0.10.
- **Implied mediation shares** (Table A-4 first stage × Table 7 politics→tax): vote share: 0.01 × −0.66 ≈ −0.007 (~1% of −0.53); ideology-H: 0.18 × −0.22 ≈ −0.040 (~8%); ideology-S: 0.23 × −0.29 ≈ −0.067 (~13%). **Observed political channels ≤ ~13%; ≥ ~87% of the effect flows through channels the paper does not observe** ("lobbying, donor pressure, agenda-setting," p.24 — named but never measured).
- The unexplained residual is observationally equivalent to **differential state trends**: with state-specific linear trends (Fig 4) the corp&union effects "are substantially attenuated and move much closer to zero" (p.15). Context: the 2010 election was the largest state-legislative realignment in 50+ years (GOP +720 seats, 20 chambers flipped — NCSL's Tim Storey; CSG) — a national tea-party wave coincident with treatment.
- **Fig 6 union-counterweight story is a group-mean artifact.** Computed from the paper's own source (Hirsch–Macpherson unionstats.com, state_2009 file, all-sector membership density 2009):
  - corp-only mean **12.7%**; corp&union mean **11.5%**; never-ban controls in sample **11.5%**; all-state mean 11.3%.
  - The corp&union group is bimodal: AK 22.3, MI 18.8, RI 17.9, WI 15.2, PA 15.0, OH 14.2 (six states ≥ national average) vs NH 10.8, WY 7.7, ND 6.8, AZ 6.5, OK 5.7, TX 5.1, **NC 3.1 (lowest in the nation — BLS 2009)**.
  - Corp-only is also spread: CT 17.3 … KY 8.6, **TN 5.1** (tied with Texas).
  - Driver states: NC 3.1 (fits "weak unions"), ND 6.8 (fits), **OH 14.2 — above the national average and above 5 of the 8 corp-only states** (does not fit; and Ohio unions outspent the corporate side 4:1 in the state's biggest post-CU policy fight).
  - The counterweight logic predicts the moderator is union density itself, not ban type. The paper never interacts treatment with pre-2010 union density.
- **What DOES line up is partisan control** (paper's own Table C-2: Dem trifecta → top-1 +0.33**, Rep governor → −0.25*): corp-only states under GOP control behaved exactly like the corp&union drivers — **Tennessee** (corp-only, union density 5.1%) enacted a 6-year phase-out of the Hall tax (a tax on interest and dividends ONLY, i.e., maximally top-concentrated) signed May 13, 2016, fully repealed Jan 1, 2021 — inside the sample; **Kentucky** (corp-only) replaced its 6-bracket, top-6.0% tax with a flat 5.0% effective Jan 1, 2018 (HB 366). The corp-only group's positive coefficient survives only because Democratic-controlled CT (+2009/2011/2015 hikes to 6.99%) and MN (+9.85% fourth tier, 2013) outweigh them.
- **Fig 7 is partly circular**: Fraser EFNA Area 2 ("Taxes") contains **2Bi: top income tax rate and top income tax threshold** — the paper's outcome variable replotted; Area 3 contains **3Aiii: union density** — the paper's Fig 6 variable. The Cato regulatory index (panel b) excludes fiscal policy but includes labor-market policy (right-to-work: MI Dec 2012, WI 2015 — the same anti-union legislation driving Fig 6's post-2010 divergence).

## 4. Theory of change in the driver states (everything below verified)

Because outcomes are TAXSIM simulations on a FIXED 2011 SCF sample, every unit of the estimate is mechanically attributable to specific statutes. The paper never names one. The decomposition:

**Mechanically possible sources within the corp&union group**: NC, OH, ND, RI, OK, WI, MI (flat), and nothing else — PA is a flat 3.07% all years (progressivity variation impossible), AK/TX/WY have no income tax, NH taxes only interest/dividends (phase-out began after 2021). So 4 of 13 "treated" states cannot respond on the outcome at all (cf. Table B-1: dropping no-income-tax states raises |effects| ~45%: top-1 −0.53→−0.77).

| State | Statutory path (top marginal rate) | Fits CU story? |
|---|---|---|
| **NC** | 3 brackets 6.0/7.0/7.75% through 2013 → flat 5.8% (2014), 5.75% (2015) — HB 998 "Tax Simplification and Reduction Act," enacted July 23, 2013 | **YES — the clean case.** 2010: of $2.6M nonparty outside money in NC legislative races, 75% came from three Art Pope-linked groups (Real Jobs NC, Civitas Action, AFP); Pope-related money >$2.2M; GOP won 18 of 22 targeted races and both chambers for the first time since the 1800s (Facing South explainer, Oct 2011; REDMAP also targeted NC). Then the 2013 flat tax under the new majority. Direct → elected Republicans → tax bill. |
| **ND** | 5.54% (2008) → 4.86% (**May 2009, retroactive to Jan 1 2009 — BEFORE Citizens United**; Tax Foundation 2009 table fn.(gg)) → 3.99% (2011) → 3.22% (2013) → 2.9% (2015) | **NO — oil, and a pre-trend.** Cuts began in the "first bloom of the oil boom" and continued 2011/2013, halving the tax (Minot Daily News); Bakken-driven reserves hit $2B by mid-2013 (Governing). Treatment coding: ND compliant Jan 22, 2010 — after its first cut was already law. |
| **OH** | 5.925% (2010–2013, end of the 2005 HB 66 21%-cut era) → 5.39% (2014; HB 59's 10%-over-3-years) → 4.997% (2015; HB 64 signed June 30, 2015) | **PARTIAL.** Fits "GOP trifecta cuts taxes" (Kasich 2011+). But Ohio's income-tax cutting began in 2005 (pre-CU trajectory), and Ohio's unions were the biggest spender in the state's marquee post-CU fight (Issue 2, $30M vs ~$8M) — they won the referendum yet couldn't stop the tax cuts, i.e., legislative majorities, not spending balance, decided tax policy. |
| **RI** | 9.9% (2010) → 5.99% (2011). H 8196 passed **unanimously** June 4, 2010, signed by Gov. Carcieri, effective Jan 1, 2011 — **five weeks BEFORE Rhode Island's CU compliance date (July 13, 2010, paper Table 2)** | **NO — cannot be a CU effect.** Enacted by one of the most Democratic legislatures in the country as a competitiveness reform, designed around the pre-existing alternative flat-tax method (~6%) that it abolished — so the effective top-rate cut was smaller than statutory, but whatever TAXSIM picks up loads onto the post-2010 treated coefficient. |
| **WI** | 7.75% → 7.65% + bracket consolidation (2013 Act 20) | Weak effect, modest cut — despite Walker and $137.5M of recall spending, the top rate barely moved. |
| **OK** | 5.50% → 5.25% (2012) | Small; GOP trifecta. |
| **MI** | flat 4.35% → 4.25% (Oct 2012) | Tiny; flat structure limits progressivity movement. |
| **AZ** | 4.54% throughout 2011–2021 window (2.5% flat enacted June 2021, effective after the sample) | ~Nothing in-sample. |

**Counter-cases (corp-only, Democratic control)**: CT top rate 5.0% → 6.5% (Sept 2009, retroactive to Jan 2009 — pre-CU), 6.7% (2011), 6.99% (2015) — raised 2003/2009/2011/2015 overall; MN added a 9.85% fourth tier at $152,540/$254,240 (May 2013, Dayton). These two drive the corp-only group's positive coefficient (paper p.19: CT and MN "consistently stand out"). Note the union-counterweight story predicts ZERO effect in corp-only states (balanced competition), not tax INCREASES; what predicts increases is Democratic trifectas (Table C-2).

**Direct vs indirect influence**: The verified NC chain is INDIRECT — money elected a new majority, the majority passed the 2013 reform. There is no documented episode of post-CU IEs lobbying a specific tax bill in any of these states, and the mediation analysis (§3) says the indirect electoral channel carries ≤~13% of the estimated effect. The paper is left attributing ~90% of its effect to an influence channel with no observable footprint in its data.

## 5. The lede (Max's point a)

The abstract claims "lifting restrictions on independent expenditures led to larger reductions in top income tax rates and a decline in overall tax progressivity than in control states" — unconditionally. In the estimates this is true ONLY where the union ban was also lifted (Table 4: corp&union top-1 −0.53**, RS −0.11**); corporate-only states show the OPPOSITE sign (+0.30, +0.07, n.s.); the between-group difference (−0.83**) is the paper's actual robust result. The corp&union group: 13 states, 4 with no income tax, effect concentrated in NC/ND/OH (Fig 5, p.19 text), of which only NC survives scrutiny as a CU story (§4).

Reconciliation with prior work: **Slattery, Tazhitdinova & Robinson (2023, J. Pub. Econ. 221:104859)** — 21 treated vs 27 control states, pooled — found "**no statistically or economically significant effects** of independent political contributions on tax outcomes," explicitly including the **top personal income tax rate** (authors' summary, Harvard Law corpgov blog, Aug 31, 2022). The Rebechi paper cites this as finding "only a moderate effect of CU" on corporate taxation (fn.5) — a mischaracterization. The reconciliation is arithmetic: pooling an opposite-signed +0.30 (8 states) and −0.53 (13 states) yields ≈ 0 — the pooled null IS the prior literature's finding. Everything therefore rides on interpreting the corp-only vs corp&union contrast causally (union-ban effect) rather than as selection: corp&union states were disproportionately Republican-controlled after 2010 (paper's own Fig C-1) and sat in the path of the 2010 wave (GOP +720 seats nationally), while the corp-only group happens to contain CT/MN/MA Democratic trifectas. The paper's residualization cannot fix this because partisan control is a post-treatment mediator AND a confounder of ban-type selection (bans were adopted decades earlier in different political eras — the paper itself notes heterogeneity sources "including differences in corporate exposure and union strength, government regulations, and different political histories and partisan control," p.3, quoting Abdul-Razzak).
