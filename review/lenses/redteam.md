# Red team: overclaiming and internal consistency — Rebechi, Van Kerm, Paradowski, Lepinteur & Rohde (2026), "What Can Money Buy?"

Draft of 31 Jul 2026 (IARIW). Page numbers are the paper's own (title page = p.1 of PDF; main text pp.1–31; appendices A–D). Every external source below was verified this session via WebSearch/WebFetch unless marked UNVERIFIED.

---

## Tier 1 — would change a reader's takeaway

### 1. The abstract claims a pooled treated-vs-control effect that no table reports, and buries the sign flip by ban type
- Abstract (p.1): findings "suggest that lifting restrictions on independent expenditures led to larger reductions in top income tax rates and a decline in overall tax progressivity than in control states."
- No pooled treated-vs-control estimate for any tax outcome exists anywhere in the paper. Tables 4, 5, 6, 8, B-1, B-2, C-1, D-1, D-2, D-3 are all disaggregated by ban type. (Pooled estimates exist only for *political* outcomes, Appendix A.)
- Table 4 (p.16): corp-only ban states: ATR top1 +0.30 (0.30), RS +0.07 (0.07) — positive, n.s. Corp&union states: top1 −0.53** (0.24), RS −0.11** (0.04). Difference: top1 −0.83** (0.35), RS −0.18** (0.07). The estimated *sign* differs by ban type and the between-group difference is itself significant — heterogeneity is the paper's actual headline result, not a nuance.
- An implied 21-state weighted pooled effect (13×corp&union + 8×corp-only) is ≈ −0.21pp for top-1% ATR and ≈ −0.04 for RS — roughly 40% of the corp&union coefficient, and almost surely insignificant given SEs of ~0.2–0.35. The abstract's sentence therefore describes an estimate the paper never ran and would likely not survive if run.
- "top income tax rates" also mislabels the outcome: these are simulated *average* tax rates of top income/wealth groups on a fixed national sample, and the paper explicitly declines to study statutory parameters ("such as the top marginal tax rate", p.9). A journalist reading the abstract would report "CU cut top tax rates," which no table shows.
- Fair play: the Discussion (p.26) states the corp-only "opposite effect... though it is not statistically significant," and the Conclusion (p.28) does condition the decline on "states that lifted bans on both corporations and union outside spending." The hedges exist in the back; the abstract has none of them.
- Severity: HIGH. Conclusion impact 3.

### 2. The asserted mechanism (rightward political shift → regressive taxes) is contradicted by the paper's own by-ban-type and residualization tables
- Abstract (p.1): "We argue that the increased conservatism in state legislatures and the strengthening of the Republican Party following the rulings have shaped fiscal policy..." Conclusion (p.28): "This political realignment translated into some measurable fiscal consequences" ... the ruling reduced top rates "both directly and indirectly, by shifting the political landscape."
- Table A-4 (p.A-8), the by-ban-type political results under the same sample restriction as the main model: Republican vote share corp-only +0.05 (0.03) n.s., corp&union +0.01 (0.02) n.s.; median ideology House corp-only +0.27**, corp&union +0.18 n.s.; Senate +0.25* vs +0.23 n.s.; all between-group differences n.s. The statistically detectable rightward shift is in the *corp-only* group — exactly the group whose taxes did NOT fall (point estimates positive). The corp&union group, which drives all tax results, shows no significant political shift on any measure.
- Table 8 (p.25) residualization: after partialling Republican vote share out of the tax outcomes, the corp&union top-1% effect is −0.56** vs the −0.53** baseline — *larger*, i.e., ≈0% mediated; ideology/polarization panels give −0.47* to −0.51**, i.e., ≈4–11% mediated. Yet the text (p.24) claims "the residualized coefficients are generally smaller, with Republican vote share remaining largely unchanged, indicating that these observable political factors account for a portion of the overall effect." For the headline outcome the Panel-A coefficient moved the wrong direction; "account for a portion" is generous for ≤11%.
- The paper itself concedes on p.24 that effects "are unlikely to be explained solely by these observed political channels" and points to lobbying/donor pressure/agenda setting — but the abstract and conclusion still lead with the electoral-realignment story.
- Related circularity in the Discussion's corroborating evidence: Figure 7(a) shows the Fraser EFNA index rising more post-2010 in corp&union states as evidence of "a more market-oriented policy environment" (p.26), but the paper's own footnote 9 says the index's three components are "government spending, taxation, and labor market freedom" — taxation, the outcome variable, is a direct component of the corroborating index. Panel (b) (Cato/Ruger–Sorens regulatory index, which excludes taxes) is the non-circular check, and there all three groups *fall* after 2010 with corp&union merely falling least — a much weaker pattern than the text's "similar pattern emerges" suggests.
- Severity: HIGH. Conclusion impact 3.

### 3. "Most research to date has found no impact on actual policy" — contradicted by an uncited APSR paper using the same natural experiment, and by the paper's own description of Slattery et al.
- Conclusion (p.28): "Most research to date has found no impact on actual policy. In contrast, we find evidence of a persistent impact on taxation policy and, consequently, on inequality."
- Gilens, Patterson & Haines (2021), "Campaign Finance Regulations and Public Policy," APSR 115(3):1074–1081 — NOT cited anywhere in the paper — uses the identical natural experiment (CU forcing 23 states to drop IE bans) with generalized synthetic control and finds treated states cut top corporate income tax rates: ATT −2.83 (SE 1.06, p=.01), ≈4% of the average rate, and −5.55 (p=.001), ≈8%, in corporate-only-ban states; plus significant pro-defendant tort-law shifts; nulls only for non-corporate policies (abortion, guns, eminent domain). Verified from the full text (open access, doi:10.1017/S0003055421000149).
  - GPH's H3 is the mirror image of this paper's union-counterweight story: "Because unions typically provide a counter-balance," GPH predicted (and for corporate taxes found) *stronger* pro-corporate effects where only corporations had been banned — since unions in those states were already free to spend and gained nothing from CU, the *relative* legal shock favoring corporations is largest there. Rebechi et al. find the opposite pattern (effects only in corp&union states) and interpret it with the same counterweight premise. Two papers, one premise, opposite predicted and estimated heterogeneity — worth an explicit reconciliation, especially since the outcome differs (corporate rate vs personal-income-tax progressivity).
- Slattery, Tazhitdinova & Robinson (2023), JPubE 221:104859 (NBER w30352): abstract states they "are not able to identify economically or statistically significant effects of corporate independent expenditures on state tax policy, including tax rates, discretionary tax breaks, and tax revenues." The paper's footnote 5 (p.9) instead describes this as "finding only a moderate effect of CU," and the intro (p.2) says Slattery examines "the impact of Citizens United on state tax policy, including personal income tax rates... finding..." — a reported precise null is upgraded to "a moderate effect" in the front of the paper, while the conclusion (p.28) claims the literature found "no impact." Both characterizations cannot be right; each is deployed where it flatters the contribution.
- Also relevant: per GPH's literature review, Abdul-Razzak, Prato & Wolton (2020) — this paper's key predecessor — did examine one policy outcome (intergovernmental transfers) and found no significant effects, consistent with "no impact," but GPH (2021) then found impacts on corporate-relevant policy. "Most research to date" as of 2026 is at best contestable and omits the closest antecedent for the paper's novelty claim.
- Severity: HIGH. Conclusion impact 3 (novelty and framing of the contribution).

### 4. Robustness language outruns the results: every estimator that relaxes parallel trends loses group-level significance, the "alternative estimator" check is a mechanical identity, and pre-trend evidence for the headline outcome is marginal
- (a) SDID (Table 6, p.20): corp&union top1 −0.40 (0.25) n.s., RS −0.07 (0.05) n.s., top5 −0.29 (0.19) n.s. Only the between-group differences reach 10% (top1 −0.72*, RS −0.13*), and the table's own note says those SEs are "computed under the assumption of independence across estimates and should therefore be interpreted as approximate." The text calls this "The results broadly confirm our main findings" (p.19) — signs, yes; significance, no.
- (b) State-specific linear trends (Fig 4, p.18): the corp&union effects are "substantially attenuated and move much closer to zero" (authors' words, p.15). Unremarked: in the same figure the corp-only *positive* estimates become larger and more persistent (top-1% panel rises to ≈+0.75 around 2015). The trends spec is read asymmetrically — its damage to the preferred result is excused (trends "may also absorb genuine treatment-related variation"), its amplification of the inconvenient positive effect is not mentioned.
- (c) Wooldridge check (p.15: results "also confirmed using an alternative TWFE estimator... Wooldridge (2025)... Table C–1"): Table C-1 (and D-2) report coefficients identical to Table 4 (and D-1) to the second decimal (0.01/0.14/0.30/... and −0.01/−0.33/−0.53/...). This is not confirmation — it is an identity. Wooldridge (2025, Empirical Economics 69(5):2545–2587, verified) proves the two-way Mundlak regression is numerically equivalent to TWFE; the procedures only diverge under staggered adoption, and here every state is treated at the same date (2010). A robustness check that cannot fail by construction should not be reported as independent support.
- (d) Pre-trends: the text claims "no evidence of systematic divergence prior to 2010" (p.15) and repeats it for Appendix D (p.D-1: "we fail to reject the null"). Table 5 (p.17): the joint pre-test for RS in corp&union states is p=0.102 — a hair above 10% for the headline progressivity outcome. Table D-3 (p.D-6): the same test in the after-federal-tax specification is p=0.085, i.e., *rejected at the 10% level*, directly under a sentence saying they fail to reject. Given low power of pre-trend tests with 13 treated clusters, "no evidence" is the wrong summary for the one outcome carrying the paper.
- (e) Verbal inflation in the main results: "with many coefficients statistically significant" (p.14) = 3 of 6 (one only at 10%). "Persistent impact" (p.28) rests on the TWFE/event-study path; under SDID and trends the persistence is not statistically distinguishable from zero at the group level.
- Severity: HIGH. Conclusion impact 2.

## Tier 2 — material tensions and framing

### 5. "Driven by few outlying states" (Discussion) vs "systematic pattern across treated states" (Appendix B) — both sentences cannot summarize the same evidence
- Discussion (p.27): "Most of our results are driven by few outlying states – Minnesota and Connecticut among the corporate ban only states and on the other extreme North Carolina, North Dakota and Ohio..."
- Appendix B (p.B-1) on the leave-one-out exercise: effects "do not depend on any single treated state or idiosyncratic policy environment, but instead reflect a systematic pattern across treated states."
- Both can be technically true only because LOO removes one state at a time; it never removes NC+ND+OH jointly, though the Discussion names exactly that trio as the drivers. With 13 treated states, dropping 1 of 3 drivers moves the mean modestly by construction. The informative check — jointly excluding the named drivers, or reporting the corp&union estimate for the other 10 states — is absent.
- The named drivers align with the confound the design cannot separate: Fig C-1 (p.C-3) shows corp&union states "disproportionately Republican-controlled," and the SDID drivers NC and ND were "predominantly under Republican control during the post-Citizens United period" (p.19). NC's 2013 flat-tax reform and ND's oil-boom-era rate cuts are visible as the step-downs in Fig C-4(b) (p.C-5). The residualization (Table 8) partials out vote share/ideology/polarization but conspicuously not the party-control variables (trifecta, governor, legislative control) that Table C-2 (p.C-9) shows are the strongest tax predictors (Dem trifecta +0.33**, Rep governor −0.25* on top-1% ATR).
- Severity: MEDIUM. Conclusion impact 2.

### 6. One number narrated as two findings; "post-government inequality" and "state tax systems" oversell what is simulated
- By construction (eq. 4, p.11; eq. 6, p.11), the pre-tax Gini is identical across all 900 state-year datasets (same fixed national SCF sample; CPI rescaling is scale-invariant, so Gini(X) never moves). Hence RS ≡ constant − Gini(post) exactly: "reduced progressivity" and "higher post-tax inequality" are the same regression up to sign. The text nonetheless lists them as separate results (p.14: "lower top-group tax rates, reduced progressivity, and higher post-tax inequality"; likewise abstract and conclusion). Figures C-7 and C-8 are mirror images presented as two exhibits.
- B ≡ 0: eqs. (1), (4), (6) carry benefit entitlements B_i, and the intro (p.1) hypothesizes "less generous social benefits," but TAXSIM simulates income tax liabilities only; no benefit is ever computed and the paper never says so explicitly. "Post-government income inequality" (p.1) and "post-government inequality" (p.28) is, in implementation, post-state-personal-income-tax inequality of one fixed national sample — no transfers, no sales/excise/property taxes (NC's 2013 reform paired income-tax cuts with sales-tax base broadening, so single-instrument coverage can misstate net progressivity changes), and no actual state populations (the public SCF has no state identifiers; the design deliberately holds the population fixed — legitimate as policy simulation, but then "inequality across US states" language should be tempered).
- Promised-but-absent outcomes: the β progressivity measure of eq. (5) (top-20 minus bottom-20 ATR) and the age-of-head group ATRs (p.10) appear in no table.
- Overall ATR effects are ~zero (Table 4: +0.01/−0.01) — of the intro's three hypothesized margins (size, progressivity, benefits), one is null, one untested, and only progressivity delivers; the null on the aggregate burden is never surfaced as a finding.
- Severity: MEDIUM. Conclusion impact 2.

### 7. Verifiable factual/citation errors in the institutional narrative and reference list
- "By November 2010, all states with pre-existing restrictions had revised their laws to comply" (p.6) is contradicted twice: (i) their own Table 2 (p.7) lists New Hampshire as N/A ("an exact date... could not be established"); (ii) Montana — Table 2's "October 18, 2010" is a state district-court ruling that the Montana Supreme Court *reversed* on Dec 30, 2011, upholding the ban; it fell only with the U.S. Supreme Court's summary reversal in American Tradition Partnership v. Bullock, June 25, 2012 (verified: SCOTUSblog/Brennan Center/FEC). Montana is thus miscoded as treated ~2 years early (it is a corp-only state, so this attenuates toward the corp-only null, but the sentence is simply wrong as history).
- Adeel et al. (2020), cited on p.A-3 as consistent with "the CU reform has increased the ideological conservatism within state legislatures," is "COVID-19 Policy Response and the Rise of the Sub-national Governments," Canadian Public Policy 46(4):565–584 (verified) — a COVID-19 protective-policy-index paper with no connection to Citizens United. Intended cite is almost surely Abdul-Razzak et al. (2020).
- Caughey & Sekhon "(2017)" — cited three times (pp.22, A-7, A-9) for partisan control predicting policy adoption "particularly for policies affecting tax burdens and welfare benefits" and for the parties' ideological divergence — is actually Caughey & Sekhon (2011), Political Analysis 19(4):385–408, an RDD-validity methods paper on close U.S. House races that supports none of those claims (verified). The intended reference is almost surely Caughey, Warshaw & Xu (2017), "Incremental Democracy: The Policy Effects of Partisan Control of State Government," Journal of Politics 79(4) (verified to exist and to make exactly those claims).
- "in Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce, the Supreme Court legislated to limit corporate independent expenditures" (p.3): courts do not legislate; Austin *upheld* Michigan's statutory restriction — as the paper's own Figure 1 correctly states ("Allowed limits on corporate independent expenditures").
- These are fixable in an afternoon, but in a paper whose contribution is institutional-legal, they are the first things a referee checks.
- Severity: MEDIUM. Conclusion impact 1 (credibility, not estimates).

## Additional notes (not in top findings)

**Sample bookkeeping.** N = 846 = 47 units × 18 years, but the text says "46 states" (p.12) and never mentions DC; a DC panel is visible in Fig C-2(c) (p.C-4). 50 − CO − SD − NE − LA = 46 states + DC = 47. SDID Ns confirm 26 never-ban control units (8+26=34×18=612; 13+26=39×18=702). "about a 20-year period" for 2004–2021 (18 years).

**Event-study window.** Leads/lags −6..+10 cover 2004–2020; 2021 (j=+11) is unaccounted for — binned into +10 or silently dropped, never stated. If 2021 observations enter with all event dummies zero, treated 2021 outcomes would be pooled with the omitted 2009 baseline — worth asking.

**Treatment definition wording.** Eq. (7) text says CU_t = 1 "after 2010" while Table 2's note codes NH "treated from 2010 onward" and event studies treat 2010 as j=0; presumably 1{t ≥ 2010}, but the text is ambiguous.

**"Opposite effect" language.** p.19 describes CT/MN as "driving the counterintuitive results of CU leading to an increase in taxation of high income or high wealth individuals in that treatment group" — an n.s. +0.30/+0.32 read as a result; the Discussion (p.26) does hedge it ("not statistically significant"). Symmetric skepticism would treat both groups' point estimates with the same caution.

**Figure 6 (union density, p.27) vs text (p.26).** Text: corp-only states historically more unionized (true — red line highest pre-2010); "gap... appears to widen in the years immediately following the ruling, before converging again around 2020" — the convergence is a one-year crash of the corp-only line at 2019 meeting the others, followed by *re-divergence* through 2024. Unstated and more interesting: the corp&union (green) line sits *above* the no-ban (blue) controls pre-2010 and falls below them after — union density itself declined post-treatment in corp&union states (MI right-to-work 2012, WI Act 10 2011 are corp&union states), i.e., the "pre-existing union strength" variable is an endogenous mediator, not a fixed characteristic.

**"87% vs 12%" (p.26).** OpenSecrets 2023–24 labor-sector split ~90% to Democrats — the figure checks out and OpenSecrets describes it as stable for two decades, so the anachronism (2023–24 cycle cited for 2010-era mechanism) is cosmetic; note it is a *federal* contributions split used for state-level IE reasoning.

**Table 1 (p.7).** Federal outside spending, for/against party — not decomposed by spender (unions vs corporations), and federal spending has no state variation; fine as motivation, loose as evidence that "unions and corporations" drove it. Ratios check out (1062/60≈18, 763/23≈33, 1411/4≈353, 923/7≈132).

**Table 3 (p.8).** 2010 percentages (77.3/20.1/2.7) don't match the rounded dollar figures (48/13/2 of 63 → 76.2/20.6/3.2) but are consistent with unrounded values (e.g., 48.4/12.6/1.7), so not provably wrong; text says 62.2% where the table says 62.3% (2024 some-disclosure).

**Tables A-11/A-12 (p.A-12).** Minority-party ideology col (1) = 0.245 (0.152), identical to the majority-party col (1) cell, then flips to −0.422*** in col (2) — almost certainly a copy-paste from A-11. Table A-14's observations row is garbled; Table A-1 upper-chamber Ns are 731/731/737 (why does the trends spec add 6 obs?).

**A.5 overreach (p.A-10).** "confirm the systematic electoral advantage the Republican Party has gained" rests on Dem legislative control −0.210** in 1 of 6 specs, Rep trifecta +0.150* in 1 of 6, and seat-share imbalance n.s. in all 6.

**Garbled tax definitions (p.11).** "Taxable Income is computed as AGI minus state income tax liability (itemized deductions) and qualified business deductions" — conflates the SALT itemized deduction with the deduction structure; "After-Tax Income" defined as *taxable* income minus federal tax and FICA is nonstandard and, if literal, makes Appendix D's pre-tax base (After-Tax Income + state taxes) exclude deduction amounts. Worth asking what TAXSIM variables were actually used (v10? v18? siitax?).

**SCF vintage.** "the LWS version of the SCF for the year 2011" (p.10) — the Fed's SCF is triennial (…2007, 2010, 2013…); there is no 2011 survey. Presumably the LWS label for the 2010 wave / 2010 incomes (eq. 6 uses X^2010). UNVERIFIED which wave/income year; worth a clarifying question, since CPI-indexing the whole panel hangs off it.

**Terminology/typos betraying draft status.** "the Supreme Court legislated" (p.3); "these two sentences" for rulings (p.1); "upper chambers of Congress" for state senates (p.A-1); "proxies by union density" (p.26); "The empirical analysis confirm" (p.28); "Tthe graphs" (p.10); axis label "Reynolds-Smolensy" (Figs C-9/C-10); figure notes claiming "state-year fixed effects" in dynamic DiD figures A-2/A-5 (would be collinear with the regressors; presumably state and year FE); Stansel et al. (2025) titled "economic freedom of north america 2022"; references "Raja and Schaffner (2015)" and "La Raja and Schaffner (2014)" alphabetized inconsistently (same authors, R vs L).

**Fair-play ledger (things the authors do hedge).** Corp-only insignificance stated in Discussion; conclusion conditions the progressivity decline on corp&union states; SDID independence-assumption caveat in Table 6 note and text; state-trends caveat framed both ways (attenuation acknowledged); "Although descriptive" for the partisan-control pattern (p.19); Table 2's NH footnote exists (the problem is the p.6 sentence ignoring it); the 87% figure is accurate.
